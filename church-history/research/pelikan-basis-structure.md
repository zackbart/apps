# Pelikan as the basis: structure and periodization

Prepared October 14, 2026. Analysis and proposal only. No data or code files were changed.

Question: what would the map's skeleton look like if Jaroslav Pelikan's *The Christian Tradition* (5 vols, 1971 to 1989) were the whole basis, and not just a citation check?

## Short answer

1. Pelikan tells one story of doctrine that runs in parallel lanes, not a tree of denominations. He splits it East and West after 600, and he splits the West again by confession after 1517. After 1700 he stops tracing bodies and tracks themes that cross all churches.
2. The map's biggest structural mismatch is the trunk. It forks the shared church into Catholic and Orthodox at 1054. Pelikan says plainly that 1054 is not the break. He describes a slow parting from the 800s to the failed reunion at Florence in 1439.
3. The second biggest mismatch is weight. Pelikan gives two whole volumes to 600 to 1300 (vol 2 East, vol 3 West). The map has 15 nodes there, and no chapter covers 1120 to 1480 at all. That is exactly when the West fixed the seven sacraments and transubstantiation, which matters most for an app about the sacraments.
4. The third is the modern period. The map has 40 nodes after 1700, most of them founding dates of denominations. Pelikan covers almost none of those bodies. He even says denominational splits often came from "nontheological factors". The fix is a two-layer model: Pelikan drives the chapters, the trunk, and the families, and a separate, clearly labeled layer of church bodies hangs off it with its own sources.
5. For display, a scrolling story with a pinned map that builds up (option B) fits Pelikan best.

## Method, and what is verified

- Chapter titles and page ranges come from each volume's contents page. I read those directly.
- Five readers, one per volume, read each volume's preface and introduction in full, plus every chapter opening, every section opening, and every chapter ending. They read more where a question needed it and checked each volume's index. Each reader confirmed the offset between PDF pages and printed pages from the running headers.
- I then checked about 50 of their quotes against the text files with a search script. Every checked quote appears on the printed page given. One OCR header misreads a "7" as a "1" (vol 3, p. 7). Quotes in this report are those I checked myself.
- Claims marked **(inferred)** are my reading or a design judgment, not something Pelikan states.
- Page numbers are printed pages. Citations use the form "Pelikan vol N, ch. 'Title', p. X".
- Copyright: no quote here is longer than one short sentence.

## 1. Pelikan's own structure

### How he defines the subject

- Doctrine is "what the church of Jesus Christ believes, teaches, and confesses on the basis of the word of God" (Pelikan vol 1, ch. 'Some Definitions', p. 1). He compresses it on p. 3: "Doctrine is what is believed, taught, and confessed." Believing means worship, teaching means preaching and exegesis, and confessing means creeds and dogma (Pelikan vol 1, ch. 'Some Definitions', pp. 3-4).
- He starts after the New Testament. He studies what the church understood the apostles to teach, not what they taught (Pelikan vol 1, ch. 'Some Definitions', p. 6).
- He sets aside non-theological causes on purpose. He allows only "the briefest references" to them (Pelikan vol 1, ch. 'The Mystery of the Trinity', p. 173).
- He periodizes by who wrote theology. Bishops did from 100 to 600. Monks did in the West from 600 to 1500. University professors have since 1500 (Pelikan vol 1, ch. 'Some Definitions', p. 5).
- He plans the volumes as parallel tracks. Vol 2 covers the Greek, Syriac, and early Russian East from 600 to 1700. Vol 3 covers the Latin West from 600 to 1300. Vol 5 puts East and West back together (Pelikan vol 1, Preface, p. ix).

### Vol 1: The Emergence of the Catholic Tradition (100 to 600)

| Ch. | Title | Pages | Main movement |
|---|---|---|---|
| | Some Definitions | 1-10 | Defines doctrine, tradition, and the plan of the work. |
| 1 | Praeparatio Evangelica | 11-67 | The church defines itself against Judaism, claiming to be the "true Israel" (pp. 12-27). It also defines itself against classical thought (pp. 27-67). |
| 2 | Outside the Mainstream | 68-120 | Marcion (pp. 71-81), the Gnostics (pp. 81-97), and the Montanists (pp. 97-108) force the church to name its norms: canon, creed, and bishops in succession (pp. 108-120). |
| 3 | The Faith of the Church Catholic | 121-171 | What ordinary churches believed: hope, salvation, and the church as a means of grace. Baptism and the Eucharist are on pp. 155-171. |
| 4 | The Mystery of the Trinity | 172-225 | Arius, Nicaea and homoousios, Athanasius, the Cappadocians, and the Spirit. He calls the Trinity "the climax" of early doctrine (p. 172). |
| 5 | The Person of the God-Man | 226-277 | Rival schools of Christology, Ephesus 431, Chalcedon 451, and a "continuing debate" through Constantinople II in 553. |
| 6 | Nature and Grace | 278-331 | Augustine, the Donatists, Pelagius, the "Semi-Pelagians", and the Council of Orange in 529 (pp. 327-329). |
| 7 | The Orthodox Consensus | 332-357 | By 600, East and West each hold a settled consensus, but they are "clearly going their separate ways" (p. 340). |

Turning points, in his order: the split from Judaism, the second-century fight over norms, Nicaea, the Cappadocian settlement, Chalcedon and its aftermath, Augustine against Pelagius, and the consensus around 600.

How he frames separations:
- The early church had no "explicit unity of doctrine" (Pelikan vol 1, ch. 'Outside the Mainstream', p. 70).
- Chalcedon "settled very little in the East" (Pelikan vol 1, ch. 'The Person of the God-Man', p. 266). He calls it "an agreement to disagree" on the same page.
- Between 451 and 553 the theological gap narrowed, "though not" the church split (Pelikan vol 1, ch. 'The Person of the God-Man', p. 274).
- By 600 the excluded groups include Western ones too: Donatists, Arian Lombards, Nestorians, and Monophysites (Pelikan vol 1, ch. 'The Orthodox Consensus', p. 332).

### Vol 2: The Spirit of Eastern Christendom (600 to 1700)

| Ch. | Title | Pages | Main movement |
|---|---|---|---|
| | Ex Oriente Lux | 1-7 | The East did not stop developing doctrine after the 600s. By then "catholic orthodoxy in the East" had its own identity (p. 6). |
| 1 | The Authority of the Fathers | 8-36 | Loyalty to the fathers and the councils. Maximus the Confessor dominates. Salvation is deification. |
| 2 | Union and Division in Christ | 37-90 | Three Eastern families, each treated on its own terms: Nestorian (p. 39), Monophysite or Jacobite (p. 49), and Chalcedonian. The one-will crisis is settled in 680-681 (pp. 62-75). |
| 3 | Images of the Invisible | 91-145 | The icon dispute from the synod of 754 to the restoration in 843. Pelikan calls it "a new version of the christological debates" (p. 91). |
| 4 | The Challenge of the Latin Church | 146-198 | Rome's authority, the theological roots of the schism, and the Filioque. |
| 5 | The Vindication of Trinitarian Monotheism | 199-251 | Christian doctrine meets Judaism, dualist sects, Islam, and philosophy. |
| 6 | The Last Flowering of Byzantine Orthodoxy | 252-298 | Symeon and hesychasm, Palamas, "the final break" with the West at Lyon and Florence, the 17th-century Orthodox confessions, and Russia as "heir apparent". |

Periodization:
- The volume is "a history of those ten centuries" (Pelikan vol 2, ch. 'Ex Oriente Lux', p. 1).
- The non-Chalcedonian churches start before 600 because they need their own account (Pelikan vol 1, Preface, p. ix, and Pelikan vol 2, ch. 'Union and Division in Christ', p. 38).
- 1453 is not a doctrinal break. The continuity across it "is far more impressive than is the discontinuity" (Pelikan vol 2, ch. 'The Last Flowering of Byzantine Orthodoxy', p. 253).
- The volume ends with the 17th-century confessions and with Moscow. Moscow got its patriarch in 1589 (Pelikan vol 2, ch. 'The Last Flowering of Byzantine Orthodoxy', pp. 280-298, esp. p. 297).

### Vol 3: The Growth of Medieval Theology (600 to 1300)

| Ch. | Title | Pages | Main movement |
|---|---|---|---|
| | The Middle Ages as "Age of Faith" | 1-8 | Medieval doctrine is "a series of footnotes" to Augustine (p. 3). |
| 1 | The Integrity of the Catholic Tradition | 9-49 | Doctrine means loyalty to the fathers. Gregory I joins their ranks. Rome becomes the test of catholic belonging (p. 45). |
| 2 | Beyond the Augustinian Synthesis | 50-105 | In the Carolingian era, quoting Augustine stops settling disputes. The fights are over adoptionism, predestination (Gottschalk), and the Eucharist (Radbertus against Ratramnus, pp. 72-80). |
| 3 | The Plan of Salvation | 106-157 | The focus moves from who Christ is to what he did. Anselm's *Cur Deus homo* (1098) and Abelard. |
| 4 | The Communication of Grace | 158-214 | How grace reaches people: Mary, the saints, the real presence (pp. 184-204), and the seven sacraments (pp. 204-214). |
| 5 | The One True Faith | 215-267 | Contradictions among the fathers, the Greek schism, 12th-century sects, and Jews and Muslims. |
| 6 | Summa Theologica | 268-307 | The 13th century summarizes and systematizes more than it develops (p. 269). |

Periodization:
- The volume covers "orthodox Catholicism in the West" from the seventh century until protest and Reformation questioned it (Pelikan vol 3, ch. 'The Middle Ages as "Age of Faith"', p. 8).
- He downplays Thomas Aquinas on purpose because this is a history of doctrine, not of thought (Pelikan vol 3, Preface, p. vii, and ch. 'Summa Theologica', p. 268).
- He brings in the Crusades only as part of the meeting with other faiths (Pelikan vol 3, ch. 'The Middle Ages as "Age of Faith"', p. 7).

The sacramental turning points (Pelikan vol 3, ch. 'The Communication of Grace'):
- The 9th-century Radbertus-Ratramnus debate (pp. 72-80, in ch. 'Beyond the Augustinian Synthesis').
- Berengar and the oaths of 1059 and 1079 (pp. 186-203).
- The first use of "transubstantiation", about 1140 (p. 203).
- Lateran IV in 1215 (pp. 203-204).
- The first list of seven sacraments, about 1145, made decisive by Peter Lombard (pp. 209-210).
- The center of gravity shifts from baptism to the Eucharist (p. 205).

### Vol 4: Reformation of Church and Dogma (1300 to 1700)

| Ch. | Title | Pages | Main movement |
|---|---|---|---|
| | Reformation Defined | 1-9 | The real frame runs from 1274, the deaths of Thomas and Bonaventure, to 1685 (p. 1). He arranges the book by doctrine. Each tradition is "predominant" in one chapter: "Roman Catholic in chapter 5, Radical in chapter 6" (p. 2). |
| 1 | Doctrinal Pluralism in the Later Middle Ages | 10-68 | No single synthesis holds. Rival views of grace, the Eucharist (Wyclif), and Mary. He uses the phrase "Hussite Reformation" (p. 59). |
| 2 | One, Holy, Catholic, and Apostolic? | 69-126 | A crisis over the church: the papal schism, Hus, conciliarism, and the chalice. By 1525 there is "a new understanding of the gospel" (p. 126). |
| 3 | The Gospel as the Treasure of the Church | 127-182 | Luther and Lutheran teaching: justification, the cross, and the sacraments (pp. 176-181). |
| 4 | The Word and the Will of God | 183-244 | The Reformed: Word and Spirit, the Lord's Supper, predestination, covenant, Dort, and Westminster. He sums up the Reformed view: Luther had gone too far, and not far enough (p. 186). |
| 5 | The Definition of Roman Catholic Particularity | 245-303 | Trent turns the Western church into one confession among several. It is the only one that "eventually took the name 'Catholic'" (p. 245). |
| 6 | Challenges to Apostolic Continuity | 304-331 | The "left wing": humanists, Anabaptists and spiritualists, and antitrinitarians. Quoting Williams, he calls it "a radical break" (p. 305). |
| 7 | Confessional Dogmatics in a Divided Christendom | 332-385 | 17th-century orthodoxies in every confession, ending with Jansenism. |

Periodization:
- He calls 1517 to 1618 "a major turning point", but he places it inside the whole history (Pelikan vol 4, ch. 'Reformation Defined', p. 1).
- He sends Pietism, Puritanism, and Jansenism into vol 5 (Pelikan vol 4, Preface, p. viii).
- The Church of England is "Catholic in its polity, Reformed in its official confessional statements" (Pelikan vol 4, ch. 'Reformation Defined', p. 2). It "defied all efforts to categorize it" (Pelikan vol 4, ch. 'The Word and the Will of God', p. 184). Ch. 7 groups it as "Reformed (including Anglican)" (p. 332).
- The English "Puritan" offshoots are deferred to the final volume (Pelikan vol 4, ch. 'Confessional Dogmatics in a Divided Christendom', p. 335).

### Vol 5: Christian Doctrine and Modern Culture (since 1700)

| Ch. | Title | Pages | Main movement |
|---|---|---|---|
| | "Alas, Theology, Too" | 1-8 | He keeps listening to "the chorus rather than" the soloists (p. 6). |
| 1 | The Crisis of Orthodoxy East and West | 9-59 | Around 1700 every church feels besieged: orthodoxy "was under siege" (p. 10). Moscow excommunicates the Old Believers in 1667 (p. 9). Pietism, Jansenism, and Puritanism respond. |
| 2 | The Objectivity of Transcendent Revelation | 60-117 | The Enlightenment attacks miracle, history, and dogma. Kant closes the chapter. |
| 3 | The Theology of the Heart | 118-173 | Pietists, Moravians, Wesley, Edwards, and Catholic and Orthodox writers turn doctrine into experience. "Evangelical Perfection" (Wesley) starts on p. 146. "The Experience of the Holy Spirit" starts on p. 162. |
| 4 | Foundations of the Christian World View | 174-226 | The 19th century defends God, creation, the human person, and the Kingdom. |
| 5 | The Definition of Doctrine | 227-281 | Inspiration, papal infallibility (1870), tradition, and Newman's development of doctrine. |
| 6 | The Sobornost of the Body of Christ | 282-336 | The doctrine of the church moves to the center. "Ecumenicity was the great new fact" (p. 282). The volume ends with Vatican II. |

Periodization:
- He weighed ending at Amsterdam in 1948 or at the 1950 Assumption dogma, then chose Vatican II (Pelikan vol 5, Preface, p. vii).
- The modern period is when long-assumed doctrines were "themselves called into question" (Pelikan vol 5, Preface, p. viii).
- Within chapters he arranges by theme. Across the volume the chapters still move forward in time (inferred from chapter openings, pp. 9, 174, 282).

### Pelikan's turning points, all volumes (one line each)

1. The church parts from the synagogue and claims Israel's scriptures (vol 1, ch. 'Praeparatio Evangelica', pp. 12-27).
2. Canon, creed, and bishops answer Marcion, the Gnostics, and Montanism (vol 1, ch. 'Outside the Mainstream', pp. 108-120).
3. Nicaea and the Cappadocian settlement of the Trinity (vol 1, ch. 'The Mystery of the Trinity', pp. 200-225).
4. Ephesus, Chalcedon, and a century of continuing debate (vol 1, ch. 'The Person of the God-Man', pp. 260-277).
5. Augustine on grace, sealed at Orange in 529 (vol 1, ch. 'Nature and Grace', pp. 292-329).
6. East and West each reach a consensus by 600 and diverge (vol 1, ch. 'The Orthodox Consensus', pp. 332-357).
7. The one-will controversy, settled in 680-681 (vol 2, ch. 'Union and Division in Christ', pp. 62-75).
8. Icons, restored in 843 (vol 2, ch. 'Images of the Invisible', pp. 91-145).
9. The Eucharist debates, transubstantiation, and seven sacraments, about 830 to 1215 (vol 3, ch. 'The Communication of Grace', pp. 184-214).
10. East and West part, from Photius in the 860s to Florence in 1439 (vol 2, ch. 'The Challenge of the Latin Church', pp. 146-198, and ch. 'The Last Flowering of Byzantine Orthodoxy', pp. 270-280).
11. Late medieval crisis of the church: Wyclif, Hus, and the councils (vol 4, ch. 'One, Holy, Catholic, and Apostolic?', pp. 69-126).
12. Reformations: Lutheran, Reformed, Roman Catholic, Radical (vol 4, chs. 3-6, pp. 127-331).
13. Enlightenment doubt and the "theology of the heart" (vol 5, chs. 2-3, pp. 60-173).
14. Defining doctrine: infallibility and development (vol 5, ch. 'The Definition of Doctrine', pp. 227-281).
15. Ecumenism and Vatican II (vol 5, ch. 'The Sobornost of the Body of Christ', pp. 282-336).

## 2. Comparison with the current map

Current skeleton: 7 CHAPTERS (overview, One church 30-320, The creeds 300-480, East and West 450-1120, The Reformation 1480-1620, New branches 1580-1980, Today 1880-2026). TRUNK is apostolic, then early-church, then chalcedonian to 1054. The map has 127 nodes. By start date: 32 fall in 30-600, 15 in 600-1300, 39 in 1300-1700, and 40 after 1700. 47 nodes carry no Pelikan citation, and 37 of those start after 1700.

### (a) Major in Pelikan, missing or underweighted on the map

Chapter-level gaps:
- **No chapter covers 1120 to 1480.** Lateran IV, the seven sacraments, Florence, Wyclif, Hus, and the councils of Constance and Basel all sit between chapters. Pelikan gives this span most of vol 3 and the first two chapters of vol 4.
- **The West's own story from 600 to 1300 is almost absent.** The map has 15 nodes for 600 to 1300 in both East and West. Pelikan gives each lane a full volume.
- **Grace and Augustine get no chapter.** "The creeds" covers only the Trinity and Christ. Pelikan gives grace its own chapter (vol 1, ch. 'Nature and Grace', pp. 278-331).
- **Late medieval reform is missing as a movement.** The Reformation chapter begins with Luther in 1517. Pelikan begins with the crisis over the church in the 1300s and 1400s (vol 4, chs. 1-2, pp. 10-126).

Missing nodes, by Pelikan weight:
- Vol 1: the parting from Judaism (pp. 12-27), Irenaeus and the rule of faith (pp. 113-120), Athanasius and the Cappadocians (pp. 203-225), the Pelagian controversy (pp. 313-318), the Council of Orange 529 (pp. 327-329).
- Vol 2: Maximus the Confessor and the one-will crisis (pp. 8-36, 62-75), iconoclasm and the 843 restoration (pp. 91-145), John of Damascus (pp. 68-88, 117-133), the Persian church's synod of 486 (p. 43), Jacob Baradaeus founding a separate Jacobite hierarchy (p. 50), hesychasm and Gregory Palamas (pp. 254-270), Lucaris and the Orthodox confessions of 1638 to 1672 (pp. 280-295), the Moscow patriarchate of 1589 (p. 297).
- Vol 3: Radbertus and Ratramnus (pp. 72-80), Berengar 1050 to 1079 (pp. 184-203), Anselm's *Cur Deus homo* (p. 106), the first list of seven sacraments, about 1145 (pp. 209-210), the Cathars and 12th-century sects (pp. 231-242).
- Vol 4: conciliarism as a movement (pp. 98-110), Erasmus (pp. 306-313), the Zurich Consensus 1549 (p. 189), the Heidelberg Catechism (pp. 202-203), the Formula of Concord (pp. 182, 356-359), the antitrinitarians and Socinians (pp. 322-331), Jansenism (pp. 374-385).
- Vol 5: the Enlightenment challenge (pp. 60-117), Schleiermacher (pp. 172-174), Newman and development (pp. 265-281), the Marian dogmas of 1854 and 1950 (p. 279), Khomiakov and sobornost (pp. 287-289), the Lambeth Quadrilateral (p. 284), Barmen 1934 (pp. 290-292), and the liturgical and sacramental renewal (pp. 295-298).

### (b) Major on the map, thin or absent in Pelikan

Thin or absent before 1700:
- edict-of-thessalonica, pentarchy-forming, benedict-of-nursia: absent from Pelikan's text. Pelikan's frame for Rome's place is Petrine primacy, Gregory's "one see of Peter" (vol 1, ch. 'The Orthodox Consensus', pp. 352-355).
- charlemagne-coronation: the coronation is not mentioned. Charlemagne appears as a patron of theology (vol 3, ch. 'The Integrity of the Catholic Tradition', p. 49).
- crusades (importance 1): Pelikan uses them only as part of the meeting with Islam (vol 3, ch. 'The Middle Ages as "Age of Faith"', p. 7).
- maronite-roman-communion, union-of-brest, eastern-catholic: absent.
- moscow-orthodox-center 1448: absent. Pelikan's markers are Russia's rejection of Florence and the 1589 patriarchate (vol 2, ch. 'The Last Flowering of Byzantine Orthodoxy', p. 297).
- great-schism-1054 (importance 1, the trunk fork): one page, and that page says the date is not the break (vol 2, ch. 'The Challenge of the Latin Church', p. 147).
- diet-of-worms: one sentence (vol 4, ch. 'The Gospel as the Treasure of the Church', p. 181).
- act-of-supremacy, elizabethan-settlement, peace-of-augsburg, jesuit-approval, thirty-years-war: absent or background only (thirty-years-war: vol 4, ch. 'Reformation Defined', p. 1).

Thin or absent after 1700:
- herrnhut-renewal, aldersgate, methodist-separation: Pelikan covers the theology but not these founding events (vol 5, ch. 'The Theology of the Heart', pp. 124-130, 148).
- Absent: holiness, pentecostal, oneness-pentecostal, charismatic, adventist, millerite-disappointment, latter-day-saints, jehovahs-witnesses, salvation-army, southern-baptist, national-baptist-convention, african-methodist-episcopal, episcopal-church-us, orthodox-autocephalies, evangelical-nondenom, second-great-awakening, excommunications-lifted.
- old-catholic: covered only as Döllinger's refusal (vol 5, ch. 'The Definition of Doctrine', p. 252).
- baptist and quaker: passing mentions only (vol 5, pp. 9-10, 46, 169).
- After 1989 (after Pelikan's last volume): joint-declaration-justification, gafcon-realignment, acna, moscow-constantinople-break, orthodox-church-ukraine, united-methodist-split, global-methodist, nicaea-anniversary, christianity-today, global-south-shift.

Framed differently:
- marburg-colloquy is modeled as the schism that splits Lutheran from Reformed. Pelikan cites Marburg mainly to show broad agreement, "especially on justification" (vol 4, ch. 'The Gospel as the Treasure of the Church', p. 151). He tells the Supper divide across pp. 176-203 and dates a Reformed origin to Zwingli on its own (p. 184).
- The "New branches" chapter frames 1580 to 1980 as Protestants "renewing and dividing". Pelikan frames the 1700s as a shared crisis and a shared turn to the heart across Protestant, Catholic, and Orthodox churches (vol 5, ch. 'The Crisis of Orthodoxy East and West', pp. 9-17, and ch. 'The Theology of the Heart', p. 119).
- The current "East and West" chapter lists "language, politics, the pope's authority, and the Filioque". Pelikan argues that the causes were doctrinal: authority, the Filioque, and later the bread of the Eucharist (vol 2, ch. 'The Challenge of the Latin Church', pp. 157-198).
- The map's arc ends in fragmentation (2008 to 2024 splits). Pelikan's ends in convergence: ecumenism and Vatican II (vol 5, ch. 'The Sobornost of the Body of Christ', pp. 282-336).

### (c) Where the lineage logic conflicts with Pelikan

1. **The 1054 fork.** TRUNK ends at 1054, and catholic and eastern-orthodox both start in 1054.
   - Pelikan: "We cannot ... date the schism with any precision" (vol 2, ch. 'The Challenge of the Latin Church', p. 147).
   - The roots are in the fourth to sixth centuries, and the first open doctrinal break is Photius (same chapter, pp. 147, 158-164).
   - The "final break" is the failure of Lyon 1274 and Florence 1439, with the sack of 1204 between them (vol 2, ch. 'The Last Flowering of Byzantine Orthodoxy', pp. 270-280).
2. **Neither East nor West "starts" in 1054.**
   - Pelikan calls the West "orthodox Catholicism in the West" from the 600s (vol 3, ch. 'The Middle Ages as "Age of Faith"', p. 8). Roman obedience already defined catholic belonging then (vol 3, ch. 'The Integrity of the Catholic Tradition', p. 45).
   - The East was "catholic orthodoxy in the East" by the 600s (vol 2, ch. 'Ex Oriente Lux', p. 6).
   - A 12th-century Latin writer still called the Greeks "dissenting catholics" (vol 3, ch. 'The One True Faith', p. 230).
   - Both lanes continue the trunk.
3. **The fork really starts around 600, not 1054.** Greek and Latin Christianity were "still parts of one and the same church" yet "clearly going their separate ways" by 600 (vol 1, ch. 'The Orthodox Consensus', p. 340). His volumes split there too.
4. **The Church of the East and the Oriental Orthodox did not split in one year.**
   - The map draws one-year schisms at 431 and 451.
   - Pelikan gives 431 and 451 as the church breaks, but says the doctrinal break was "consummated a hundred years or so after the political" (vol 2, ch. 'Union and Division in Christ', p. 37).
   - The Persian church adopted its Christology in 486 (p. 43). Jacob Baradaeus's work "was the establishment of a new church" (p. 50).
   - Pelikan treats each as "a distinct branch" with its own doctrinal history (p. 49).
5. **The one-will crisis (680-681) is internal to the Chalcedonian lane.** It is not a further split (vol 2, ch. 'Union and Division in Christ', p. 62). The map handles this correctly. Keep it that way.
6. **Anglican.** The map gives a single parent, catholic, in 1534. That matches polity only. Doctrinally Pelikan files the Church of England with the Reformed (vol 4, ch. 'Reformation Defined', p. 2, ch. 'The Word and the Will of God', p. 184, and ch. 'Confessional Dogmatics in a Divided Christendom', p. 332). Its doctrinal marker is the Thirty-Nine Articles, not 1534.
7. **Anabaptist.** The map has a single parent, reformed.
   - Pelikan supports a Zurich origin. At Zofingen in 1532 the Reformed and Anabaptists agreed they were "one in the chief points" (vol 4, ch. 'Challenges to Apostolic Continuity', p. 315).
   - But his category is "Radical", a break and not a branch (pp. 305, 314). The free family's color should rest on that.
8. **Reformation start.** The map starts the Reformation at 1517. Pelikan's "Hussite Reformation" (vol 4, ch. 'Doctrinal Pluralism in the Later Middle Ages', p. 59) supports a Hussite line from about 1415. That line runs to the Unity of Brethren and later Moravians. Pelikan notes the Brethren took a Lutheran confession in 1535 and a Calvinist-leaning one in 1575 (vol 4, ch. 'The Word and the Will of God', p. 216).
9. **Catholic after Trent.** The map draws Catholic as an unchanged line. Pelikan says Trent made Rome one confessional "particularity" among others (vol 4, ch. 'The Definition of Roman Catholic Particularity', p. 245). A label change at Trent ("Roman Catholic") captures this.
10. **Baptist, Quaker, Congregationalist, Methodist.** Pelikan gives no lineage for these. Baptists, Quakers, and Congregationalists are only English "Puritan" offshoots deferred to vol 5 (vol 4, ch. 'Confessional Dogmatics in a Divided Christendom', p. 335). He treats Wesley as a voice in a cross-church movement, not as a split (vol 5, ch. 'The Theology of the Heart', p. 148). The map's parents for these (anglican, congregationalist, and so on) are not wrong. They just cannot rest on Pelikan.
11. **Small date points.**
    - Pelikan dates the Old Believers' excommunication to 1667 (vol 5, ch. 'The Crisis of Orthodoxy East and West', p. 9). The map's 1666 is defensible but should note both dates.
    - The Filioque node's 589 and 1014 dates come from other sources. Pelikan's account starts with Carolingian evidence (vol 2, ch. 'The Challenge of the Latin Church', pp. 183-187).

## 3. Gaps a history of doctrine leaves, and how to fill them

The rule for every gap: **Pelikan sets the frame. Other sources fill in facts inside it, and they are labeled.** Pelikan himself gives the license. He calls splits over "slavery and class structure" examples of the "nontheological factors" behind division (vol 5, ch. 'The Sobornost of the Body of Christ', pp. 302-303). So the map can show institutional splits without pretending they are doctrinal turning points.

Proposed mechanism (needs a schema change in src/types.ts, which I did not make):
- Add a `basis` field to every node. Values: `pelikan` (the claim and its place in the story rest on Pelikan), `pelikan-frame` (Pelikan gives the movement, and other sources give this body's facts), and `outside` (no Pelikan coverage).
- Show `outside` nodes in a muted style, with a one-line note: "Not covered by Pelikan. Source: ...".

### Trunk and branch model

Current: one trunk to 1054, then a two-way fork, then a tree of denominations.

Proposed (design inferred from the verified passages above):
1. **Prelude, 30 to 100.** "Jesus and the apostles" stays, marked as before Pelikan's story.
2. **Shared church, 100 to about 600.** One trunk. Rename "Early church" to "The shared (catholic, meaning universal) church". That follows Pelikan's vol 1 title, with a gloss so Protestant readers do not read "Catholic" as Roman.
3. **Two early branches peel off gradually.** Church of the East: a tapered connector from 431 to 486, solid after. Oriental Orthodox: tapered from 451 to the 540s (Jacob Baradaeus), solid after. Keep the 431 and 451 markers.
4. **From 600, a braided double trunk.** One Greek and one Latin lane, drawn close and tied together. The ties are shared councils, at 680-681 and 787. The gap widens at the 860s (Photius), 1054, 1204, and 1274. The braid ends after Florence (1439) is rejected. No branch "starts" in 1054. 1054 becomes a marker on the braid.
5. **Reformation as a fan from the Latin lane.** It starts with a Hussite strand in about 1415. Main strands from 1517 to 1560: Lutheran, Reformed (Anglican is drawn here doctrinally, with a dotted institutional link to the Latin lane), and Radical (Anabaptist, plus an antitrinitarian strand flagged outside the historic creeds). The Latin lane relabels to "Roman Catholic" at Trent.
6. **After 1700, families, not forks.** New bodies hang as thinner lines inside three Pelikan families. These are the Reformed and Puritan free churches, the theology of the heart (Pietist, Moravian, Methodist, then Holiness and Pentecostal, marked outside Pelikan), and those outside the historic creeds. Draw their institutional parents as thin links, sourced to the bodies themselves.

## 5. Display implications

**Recommendation: B, a scrolling story with a pinned map that builds up. Use C as the entry point into it. Do not build A as the main view.**

Why B fits Pelikan:
- Pelikan writes a narrative of development, not a diagram. Each volume's chapters are movements with a direction.
- B can show overlapping periods one after another. Chapters 3, 4, and 5 cover the same centuries in different lanes. A story can walk the East lane, then the West lane, then the parting, while the pinned map lights up the matching lane. A single static diagram cannot show that order.
- Gradual separations (431 to 486, 451 to the 540s, 863 to 1439) need time to read. A map that builds up as the reader scrolls can draw the braid widening.
- Vol 5 is thematic. Its themes cross all churches, so they read naturally as story panels that highlight many lanes at once.
- B makes it easy to show the line between Pelikan and other sources. The epilogue panel and the muted `outside` nodes can appear in the story with a plain label.

Why not A (subway map) as the main view:
- Subway maps assume clean junctions and clear "who came from whom". Pelikan rejects clean junctions at the three biggest splits (451, 1054, 1517).
- He supplies almost no lineage after 1700, which is where a subway map would be densest.
- A would push the map back toward an institutional tree, the very thing this redesign moves away from.
- A works well as a static summary (the chapter 0 overview or a printable legend), drawn with braided segments.

Where C (start from your church, compare two) fits:
- It works well as a door into B. "Start from my church" picks a lane, and the story then highlights that lane's stations.
- Comparison should run on Pelikan's doctrinal stations, not on lineage. For example: Nicaea, Chalcedon, icons 843, Berengar to Lateran IV, the seven sacraments, Marburg and the Zurich Consensus, Trent. The question at each station is "where do these two churches stand on this?"
- That matches the sacraments grid already in research/sacraments.json, and it stays neutral between traditions.
