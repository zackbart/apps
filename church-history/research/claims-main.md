# "How it sees itself" claims: the 12 main traditions

Output: `research/claims-main.json`. Rules followed: `research/CLAIMS_BRIEF.md`, `research/REVIEW_BRIEF.md`, unslop. Each `self` sentence is 22 to 24 words, with no dashes, no semicolons, and straight quotes. One short book quote per entry at most.

## How I kept the four ancient churches even

All four sentences use one frame: "Sees itself as the church Christ founded through the apostles, continuing without a break ..." followed by the one detail each church itself names as its mark of continuity:

- Church of the East: the apostles Thomas, Addai, and Mari, and the faith of Nicea.
- Oriental Orthodox: the first three councils and Cyril.
- Eastern Orthodox: apostolic succession and the seven councils.
- Catholic: bishops in communion with the pope, Peter's successor.

I left out "the one church" and "unchanged" in all four. The Orthodox (Holy and Great Council 2016) and Catholic (Lumen Gentium 8) official texts each identify their own body with the one church. Pelikan shows the Church of the East (2:39) and the non-Chalcedonians (2:50-52) made the same claim about themselves. But I could not verify a present-day official sentence of that strength for those two, so adding it to only two of the four would have tilted the set. "Unchanged" fits the Eastern self-description (Pelikan 2:13-16), but Catholic teaching speaks of doctrine developing (Pelikan 4:5, 5:265-281). Using it for three of the four would have made the Catholic line read differently. Pelikan himself notes the shared pattern. Every party in the Byzantine disputes claimed to hold the changeless truth (2:13), and in the sixteenth century everyone claimed to be catholic and evangelical (4:246). Damick ch1 makes the same point about nearly all Christian groups.

## Entries

### church-of-east: continuity
- Why: Pelikan reports the Church of the East's own view. It held the true gospel and saw Nicea and Constantinople as normative, and it read Ephesus and Chalcedon as a break by others. Its official site names the apostolic founders.
- Read: Pelikan 2:37-49 (whole section "Duality of Hypostases"), with 2:39-40 as the core. Site: assyrianchurch.org.au/the-church says the church "was born from the missionary work of the apostles, St Thomas from the Twelve, and Sts Addai & Mari from the Seventy-Two."
- `Pelikan 2:39-40`, printed page 39: "Christ our Lord, hold the truth of the gospel"
- Not verified: the site makes no explicit claim of an unchanged faith, and I did not write one. Pelikan treats Addai and Mari only through the liturgy, so the founders' names rest on the church's site.

### oriental-orthodox: continuity
- Why: Pelikan shows the non-Chalcedonians claiming an unbroken tradition from Peter and from James. They accepted the first three councils, held Cyril as the norm, and saw Chalcedon as the departure. WCC calls these churches "founded in apostolic times" and resting on the first three councils. The Coptic Church names St Mark and "unbroken 118 patriarchs."
- Read: Pelikan 2:49-62 ("One Incarnate Nature of God the Logos"), with 2:50-52 as the core.
- `Pelikan 2:50-52`, printed page 50: "from Peter, the chief of the divine band of apostles, all the way to Severus"
- Note: Pelikan's sources are mostly the West Syrian (Jacobite) church. The family also includes Coptic, Armenian, Ethiopian, Eritrean, and Malankara churches, so the WCC and Coptic pages back the family-wide wording. "Cyril" means Cyril of Alexandria. The sentence says only "Cyril" to stay within 25 words.

### eastern-orthodox: continuity
- Why: the seventeenth-century Eastern confessions define orthodoxy as the historic faith of the orthodox and catholic church. They hold that unwritten tradition came down orally from the apostles, and they accept seven councils (Pelikan 2:288-290). The 2016 Holy and Great Council calls the Orthodox Church "the One, Holy, Catholic, and Apostolic Church". It says its unity "is expressed through the apostolic succession and the patristic tradition". Damick, an Orthodox priest, reports the Orthodox view in his own church's voice: the Orthodox Church believes it is uniquely the one Church. I used him only for that.
- Read: Pelikan 2:8-16 ("The Authority of the Fathers", "The Changeless Truth of Salvation") and 2:280-295 ("The Definition of Eastern Particularity"). Damick ch1, whole section "HOW THE ORTHODOX CHURCH VIEWS THE NON-ORTHODOX".
- `Pelikan 2:288-290`, printed page 289: "others had been handed down orally from the apostles to subsequent generations of the church"

### catholic: continuity
- Why: Pelikan shows the Catholic side after the Reformation locating true catholicity under Peter and his successor (4:246). He writes that at Trent Roman Catholicism professed to keep all three criteria of apostolic continuity: Scripture, tradition, and apostolic office (5:283). Lumen Gentium 8 says the Church "subsists in the Catholic Church, which is governed by the successor of Peter and by the Bishops in communion with him". Lumen Gentium 20 says bishops "have succeeded to the place of the apostles."
- Read: Pelikan 4:1-9 ("Reformation Defined"), 4:245-248, 5:12-18, 5:282-286.
- `Pelikan 5:283`, printed page 283: "Roman Catholicism also professed to retain all three criteria of apostolic continuity"
- Note: Pelikan 5:283 goes on to report internal criticism of papal centralization. I did not use it, because it is not the self-description.

### lutheran: reform
- Why: Melanchthon stressed the continuity of the church's teaching, though not of its institutional succession, with the church of the fathers. Luther held to the ancient creeds as testimony of standing with the true church. The Augsburg Confession declared loyalty to Nicea, and the Book of Concord includes the three creeds (Pelikan 4:176-177). Pelikan 5:18 records that defenders of the Augsburg Confession accepted the label Old-Catholic Lutheranism. The Augsburg Confession, end of Part I, says its doctrine has "nothing that varies from the Scriptures, or from the Church Catholic, or from the Church of Rome as known from its writers."
- Read: Pelikan 4:167-182 ("The Centrality of the Gospel"), 4:1-9, 5:12-18. Damick ch3 "Lutheranism" in full. It does not state the Lutheran self-understanding on continuity, so I did not cite it.
- `Pelikan 4:176-177`, printed page 176: "testimony that I stand with the true Christian church"

### reformed: reform
- Why: Reformed theology made reform by the word of God its program (4:186). It claimed that genuine antiquity stood on its side and that it taught nothing other than the ancient church (4:211-212). Calvinists claimed continuity of doctrine and discipline with the ancient church (5:18). Damick ch4 "The Great Apostasy" agrees in a sentence reporting the self-view: the Magisterial Reformers saw themselves not as rediscovering the church but as repairing it. I did not cite Damick, because the rest of that section is his assessment.
- Read: Pelikan 4:183-232 ("Word and Spirit", "Obedience to the Word and Will of God"), 4:313, 5:12-18. Damick ch3 "The Reformed Churches", ch4 "The Great Apostasy".
- `Pelikan 4:211-212`, printed page 212: "that we today do not teach anything else than what was accepted then"
- No tier 3 source: Pelikan covers the claim directly. "Without later additions" rests on Bullinger's rejection of nonscriptural tradition (4:211-212).

### anabaptist: restoration
- Why: Pelikan says the Anabaptists challenged the supposed apostolic structure of the church and put a restored apostolic church life in its place (4:314). They held that the church had degenerated since the apostles and that the true church had long been lost (4:315-316). Mennonite World Conference says the early Anabaptists "would have said that they were simply trying to be faithful followers of the teachings of Jesus and the example of the early church."
- Read: Pelikan 4:304-322 (chapter 6 opening and "Spirit versus Structure").
- `Pelikan 4:314-316`, printed page 314: "a restored form of truly apostolic church life"
- Members disagree: MWC also frames Anabaptism as coming out of renewal movements within the Catholic Church and the early Reformation. Some Mennonites today would describe themselves as a renewal or a "third way". The official story still centers on following the early church, so I kept restoration.
- Not used: Pelikan 4:316 also quotes a radical who said no gathered church had existed for 1,400 years. That is Sebastian Franck, a spiritualist rather than an Anabaptist, so it is not cited for this tradition.

### anglican: reform
- Why: Jewel's Apology identified the primitive church of the apostles and fathers as the catholic church (5:15). Asked where the church was before the Reformation, his successor answered that it was in the same place, then corrupted and now pure (5:18). The Lambeth Quadrilateral sums up Anglican ecclesiology as Scripture, the two creeds, the two sacraments, and the historic episcopate (5:284-285). The Church of England's Declaration of Assent says it "is part of the One, Holy, Catholic and Apostolic Church" and bears witness to the faith in its historic formularies.
- Read: Pelikan 4:2, 4:348-349 (Hooker), 5:11-18 ("Apologia for the Church"), 5:282-286. Damick ch3 "Anglicanism and its Heirs" (via media). Not cited, because his framing is largely assessment.
- `Pelikan 5:15-18`, printed page 18: "only it was then corrupted, and it is now pure."
- Partly verified: WebFetch could not render the full Preface on the Church of England page. A printable copy of it (churchofengland.org/node/9707/printable/print) and the search results confirmed "part of the One, Holy, Catholic and Apostolic Church". I cite the canonical page URL.
- Members disagree: Anglo-Catholics stress continuity, and some evangelical Anglicans stress the Reformation. "Catholic and reformed" is the official position.

### methodist: renewal
- Why: Pelikan lists Methodism as a movement within Anglicanism, alongside Puritanism (5:37). He shows Wesley sure he agreed with the Thirty-Nine Articles, and sure that the Anglican church matched the primitive church on most points (5:124). Damick reports that Methodism began as a movement inside the Anglican church and that the later split went against the Wesleys' wishes. The World Methodist Council says Methodist churches "stand within the continuity of the one universal Church" and that "Methodists form only a part of the Church universal."
- Read: Pelikan 5:37, 5:118-127, 5:146-157, 5:171. Damick ch3 "Anglicanism and its Heirs" (Methodism subsection).
- `Damick ch3: Anglicanism and its Heirs`, quote: "Methodists began as an eighteenth-century movement within the Anglican church"
- Note: Methodists did become separate churches, but they do not claim to replace the Church of England or the wider church. That matches the brief's definition of renewal.
- Not verified: I could not confirm the wording "to reform the nation, particularly the Church" in a current official text, so I did not use it. The UMC and British Methodist pages I fetched did not include the origin wording.

### pentecostal: restoration
- Why: the first Pentecostals called themselves the Apostolic Faith Movement. They claimed the gift given at the first Pentecost was given again, with miraculous gifts reclaimed after lying dormant (Damick ch6). Faupel's review of Blumhofer's history of the Assemblies of God (Christian Century, 1990) describes the restorationist belief that "God began restoring to the church truth which had been lost". He also says Pentecostals looked to Acts "to follow the apostolic patterns of the early church."
- Read: Damick ch6 "THE LATTER RAIN", "ORIGINS IN THE HOLINESS MOVEMENT", "The Apostolic Faith Movement", "The Second Pentecost". Pelikan has no Pentecostal material. I searched vol5 for pentecost, tongues, glossolalia, Azusa, and Holiness, and found nothing on the movement. Damick is used only where he reports the movement's own claim.
- `Damick ch6: The Second Pentecost`, quote: "miraculous gifts of power are reclaimed by Christians, perhaps after having lain dormant for centuries"
- Not verified: ag.org (Statement of Fundamental Truths, History) returns 403 to WebFetch and curl. I did not cite the AG article on Spirit baptism and the "early Christian church", even though the map already cites that page elsewhere.

### baptist: restoration
- Why: Baptists define a church by the New Testament pattern. The Baptist Faith and Message 2000, Art. VI, says: "A New Testament church of the Lord Jesus Christ is an autonomous local congregation of baptized believers". Damick reports the believers' baptism reasoning from New Testament practice and the Baptist distinctives.
- Read: Damick ch4 "Baptists", "Believers' Baptism", "The Great Apostasy". Pelikan 5:9-10 is about the German Baptist Brethren, not this family. Pelikan 5:46 says only that Baptists contended for immersion as the one authentic form. Neither states a self-understanding, so I cited no Pelikan.
- `Damick ch4: Believers’ Baptism`, quote: "Thus, they reasoned that baptism should be only for those who make a clear profession of faith in Christ."
- Members disagree: (1) Landmark Baptists, a minority (Damick ch4 "The Great Apostasy"), claim an unbroken succession of true churches back to the apostles, which is a continuity claim. (2) Many Particular Baptist and British Baptist writers see Baptists as heirs of the Reformation who carried reform further. Restoration is the majority framing, but this is the least clear-cut call in the set.
- Note: the Damick sections cited report Baptist practice and reasoning. They do not state a continuity claim in the tradition's words, so the claim itself rests on BF&M.

### evangelical-nondenom: other
- Why: independent evangelical churches claim no institutional continuity, no reform of a parent church, and no renewal inside one. Most do not say that other churches lost the faith either. Their claim is to belong to the spiritual unity of all believers under the Bible's sole authority, which matches none of the four categories cleanly. Sources: the NAE Statement of Faith ("We believe in the spiritual unity of believers in our Lord Jesus Christ", and the Bible as "the only infallible, authoritative Word of God"). The Lausanne Covenant §6 ("The church is the community of God's people rather than an institution"). Damick on congregational autonomy.
- Read: Damick ch5 "The Mega-Church Movement", ch4 "The Invisible Church". Pelikan does not cover twentieth-century nondenominationalism.
- `Damick ch5: The Mega-Church Movement`, quote: "Non-denominational churches pride themselves on being completely autonomous organizations that answer to no one outside the local community."
- Caveats: NAE and Lausanne speak for evangelicals broadly, not for independent churches as a body, which have no common official text. Some independent churches describe themselves as "Acts 2" or "New Testament" churches, which leans toward restoration. I chose `other` because the claim most of them share is spiritual unity, not recovery of something lost. The rest of Damick's mega-church section is his assessment and was not used.
